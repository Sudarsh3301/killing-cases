 II.  
STANDARD ON MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT  
Summary judgment is appropriate if the pleadings, depositions and affidavits 
show that no genuine issue of material fact exists for trial and that the moving party 
is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.  Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 
322, 106 S. Ct. 2548, 2552, 91 L. Ed. 2d 265 (1986). 
The Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990, as amended by the Americans 
with Disabilities Amendments Act (collectively “ADA”), prohibits employers from 
discriminating against qualified individuals with a disability in any aspect of 
employment. 42 U.S.C. §12101 et seq. The ADA requires employers to make 
reasonable accommodations to the known physical or mental limitations of an 
otherwise qualified individual with disability unless it would cause an undue 
hardship on the employer’s business operations. 42 U.S.C. §12112(b)(5)(A). 
In this case, the Plaintiff alleges that his employer, Food City, failed to provide 
a reasonable accommodation in response to his request to bring his service dog to 
work, that Food City failed to comply with the interactive process as required under 
the ADA and that Food City retaliated against him for engaging on a protected 
activity under the ADA. (Doc. #1, Complaint, ¶¶ 30, 31). As shown below, the 
undisputed material facts and applicable authority do not support Plaintiff’s 
allegations and instead demonstrate that Food City is entitled to summary judgment 
as a matter of law as to all of Plaintiff’s claims.

III. 
APPLICABLE LAW AND AUTHORITY 
To establish a prima facie case of discrimination under the ADA based on a 
failure to provide a reasonable accommodation, the plaintiff must show: (1) he is a 
qualified individual with a disability; (2) that, with or without reasonable 
accommodations, he can perform the essential functions of the job; and (3) he was 
discriminated against because of his disability. Callahan v. Emory Healthcare, 2021 
U.S. Dist. LEXIS 39467, *46 (N.D. Ga. 2021), recommendation for summary 
judgment adopted, 2021 U.S. Dist. LEXIS 38010, 2021 WL 732352 (N.D. Ga., Feb 
10, 2021).  The ADA provides that an employer unlawfully discriminates against a 
qualified individual when the employer fails to provide a “reasonable 
accommodation” unless the accommodation would impose an undue hardship. 42 
U.S.C. §12112(b)(5)(A).  Callahan, supra, at *56, citing Anderson v. Embarq/Sprint, 
379 Fed. Appx. 924, 927, 2010 U.S. App. LEXIS 10342, *5, 23 Am. Disabilities 
Cas. (BNA) 813 (11th Cir. 2010) (An accommodation is "reasonable," and therefore 
required under the statute, "only if it enables the employee to perform the essential 
functions of the job", quoting Holly v. Clairson Indus., LLC, 492 F.3d 1247, 1256 
(11th Cir. 2007). 
The duty of an employer to engage in an interactive process originates in the 
federal regulations implementing the ADA. 29 CFR §1630.2(o)(3) states as follows:  
To determine the appropriate reasonable accommodation it may be 
necessary for the covered entity to initiate an informal, interactive process

with the individual with a disability in need of the accommodation. This 
process should identify the precise limitations resulting from the disability and 
potential reasonable accommodations that could overcome those limitations. 
 
29 C.F.R. §1630.2(o)(3) 
 
Plaintiff filed this case alleging violations of the ADA, raising issues such as 
reasonable accommodations, interactive processes and alleging discrimination and 
retaliation on the basis of Plaintiff’s disability. For purposes of this Motion, Food 
City does not contest that Plaintiff has one or more disabilities. In fact, Plaintiff was 
hired in May of 2019 with Food City’s awareness that he had a disability in that he 
did not have the use of one arm.  Plaintiff had satisfactorily performed the essential 
functions of the job of fuel clerk for two (2) years when on July 23, 2021, Plaintiff 
presented at work with his service dog. From that point forward, nothing would 
satisfy Plaintiff but that he be allowed to bring his service dog to work.  Plaintiff 
claimed that his service dog assisted him with stability issues, but during the two 
years of working as a fuel clerk for Food City, there was no indication that he needed 
accommodation for any reason. In fact, Plaintiff has admitted in his testimony that 
he advised Food City that nothing in his job had changed and that he was still able 
to perform the essential functions of his job as before – he just wanted to have his 
service dog.  
A.  PLAINTIFF DID NOT REQUIRE AN ACCOMMODATION.   
The Plaintiff herein did not require an accommodation to perform his job as 
Case 4:22-cv-00179-WMR     Document 31     Filed 04/11/23     Page 9 of 20
10 
 
fuel clerk with Food City.  He had satisfactorily performed the job for over two (2) 
years. The Eleventh Circuit Court of Appeals has held that “if an employee does not 
require an accommodation to perform (her) essential job functions, then the 
employer is under no obligation to make an accommodation, even if the employee 
requests an accommodation that is reasonable and could be easily provided.” 
D’Onofrio v. Costco Wholesale Corp., 964 F.3d 1014, 1022 (11th Cir., 2020), cert. 
denied, 2021 U.S. LEXIS 826 (U.S. Feb. 22, 2021).  
In D’Onofrio v. Costco Wholesale Corp., supra, the plaintiff alleged she had 
been discriminated against on the basis of her disability, which was deafness. The 
plaintiff began working in 1989 at Costco’s warehouse, performed satisfactorily for 
about 14 years, never had difficulty communicating and never filed any complaints 
internally. In 2012, when she began having communication difficulties with a 
supervisor, Pack, she complained and requested that Pack be transferred, and that 
Costco train all managers on deaf culture. D’Onofrio, 964 F.3d 1014, 1017. Costco 
then took significant measures to address D’Onofrio’s requests including installation 
of special equipment and conducting deaf-culture training of its managers in March 
of 2013.   
Ultimately, plaintiff rejected these steps and instead refused to communicate 
with supervisor Pack. After several work-related conduct issues, she was terminated 
in October of 2013 and brought suit under the ADA and Florida state law, which is

11 
 
analyzed under the same framework as the ADA.  
At the trial of the case, the jury found in favor of Costco on the issue of 
termination and retaliation but found that Costco had failed to accommodate her 
disability. D’Onofrio, 964 F.3d 1014, 1021. In short, the District Court granted 
Costco’s Motion for Judgment as a matter of pursuant to Fed. R. Civ. P. 50(a), and 
plaintiff appealed. The Eleventh Circuit affirmed the District Court’s decision to 
grant Costco’s motion for judgment as matter of law. D’Onofrio, 964 F.3d 1014, 
2020 U.S. App. LEXIS 20869, cert. denied, 2021 U.S. LEXIS 826 (U.S. Feb. 22 
2021). 
The D’Onofrio case is factually similar to the case before this Court because 
Plaintiff herein, like D’Onofrio, performed his job for two (2) years without incident. 
Even today, he does not allege any issues or limitations in performing the essential 
job duties of fuel clerk. Like the plaintiff in D’Onofrio, Plaintiff herein seeks one 
particular accommodation of bringing his service dog to work, claiming the service 
dog is necessary to address physical and mental limitations which Plaintiff has had 
during the entire time of his employment but which have presented no issues with 
respect to his work performance.  
Finally, as in D’Onofrio, Plaintiff herein has not actually requested an 
accommodation as such but has instead presented Food City with an ultimatum that 
he be allowed to bring his service dog to work. Such circumstances do not trigger

12 
 
ADA-related obligations for the employer because the employee is not requesting 
an accommodation that is necessary to perform his job. An accommodation is 
reasonable and necessary under the ADA only when it enables the employee to 
perform the essential functions of the position. McKane v. UBS Fin. Servs., Inc., 
363 Fed. Appx. 679, 681, 2010 U.S.App. LEXIS 1386, **4  (11th Cir. 2010); Holly 
v. Clairson Industries, LLC, 492 F.3d 1247, 1255 (11th Cir. 2007); Lucas v. W.W. 
Grainger, Inc., 257 F.3d 1249 (11th Cir. 2001).   
Under applicable Eleventh Circuit authority, the Plaintiff’s request to work 
with his service dog was not a reasonable and required accommodation under the 
ADA because it was not necessary in order for Plaintiff to perform the essential 
functions of the fuel clerk position. Based on the foregoing authority, Food City is 
entitled to summary judgment as a matter on this issue.  
B. FOOD CITY DID NOT FAIL TO ENGAGE IN THE INTERACTIVE 
PROCESS.  
Plaintiff claims Food City violated the ADA by failing to engage in the 
interactive process, however, it was Food City who initiated the interactive process 
to explore the reasons Plaintiff felt he needed to bring his service dog to work. 
Plaintiff did not request Food City to provide a reasonable accommodation to 
address a particular limitation in his job performance; rather he simply showed up 
to work on July 23, 2021 with his service dog and claimed she was necessary for his

13 
 
stability, despite the fact that there had been no performance issues in the two (2) 
years of Plaintiff’s work as a fuel clerk.   
After Plaintiff brought his service dog to work on July 23, 2021, Food City 
engaged in three (3) separate meetings with Plaintiff in an effort to gather 
information from him, understand the need for Plaintiff’s service dog and to explore 
potential alternative accommodations. Despite these efforts, as Plaintiff has testified, 
he made it clear to Food City that the only option he would consider was bringing 
his service dog to work.  At that point, it was evident that no further options were 
going to be considered by Plaintiff as alternatives to bringing his service dog to work.  
In the case of Stewart v. Happy Herman’s Cheshire Bridge, 117 F.3d 1278 
(11th Cir. 1997), the Eleventh Circuit Court of Appeals specifically noted that 
“liability simply cannot arise under the ADA when an employer does not obstruct 
an informal interactive process; makes reasonable efforts to communicate with the 
employee and provide(s) accommodations based on the information it possesses; 
and the employee's actions cause a breakdown in the interactive process. Stewart, 
117 F.3d 1278, 1287, 1997 U.S. App. LEXIS 18981 (11th Cir. 1997), citing, Beck 
v. Univ. of Wis. Bd. of Regents, 75 F.3d 1130, 1137 (7th Cir. 1996)("liability for 
failure to provide reasonable accommodations ensues only where the employer bears 
responsibility for the breakdown").       
In this case, it was clear that what Plaintiff wanted was not a “reasonable

accommodation,” nor did he want to engage in an interactive process with Food City 
to identify an alternative to bringing his service dog to work. In his deposition 
testimony, Plaintiff has admitted that he made it clear to Food City in his discussions 
with Mr. Ellison and Mr. Richards that nothing was going to satisfy him other than 
being allowed to bring his service dog to work. (Exhibit 1, Plaintiff depo., p. 75, 
lines 5-23; Ex. 3, Ellison, Depo., p. 57, lines 2-7; Ex. 4., Lyon depo., p. 42, lines 1-
13).  
Thus, the breakdown in the interactive process in this matter was not due to 
any failure on the part of Food City but rather was due to Plaintiff’s refusal to 
entertain any alternative accommodations besides bringing his service dog to work.    
 
Based on the undisputed material facts and the forgoing authority, Food City 
is entitled to summary judgment as a matter of law on this issue.  
C. PLAINTIFF WAS NOT ENTITLED TO THE ACCOMMODATION OF HIS 
CHOICE. 
Even if it is presumed that Food City was obligated under the ADA to provide 
a reasonable accommodation to the Plaintiff, the ADA does not impose liability on 
an employer for failure to provide the accommodation particularly desired by an 
employee; rather the ADA only requires the employer to provide a reasonable 
accommodation.   Stewart v. Happy Herman’s Cheshire Bridge, 117 F.3d 1278; 1997 
U.S. App. LEXIS 18981 (11th Cir. 1997).

D. THERE IS NO EVIDENCE OF RETALIATION.  
In Stewart v. Happy Herman’s Cheshire Bridge, 117 F.3d 1278 at 1287, the 
Eleventh Circuit reviewed the elements to a claim of retaliation under the ADA, 
using the same analysis as used under Title VII:  
To establish a prima facie case of retaliation, a plaintiff must 
show: (1) statutorily protected expression; (2) adverse employment 
action; and (3) a causal link between the protected expression and the 
adverse action. See Goldsmith v. City of Atmore, 996 F.2d 1155, 1163 
(11th Cir.1993) (explaining requirements to show retaliation in the Title 
VII context). Once a prima facie case is established, the burden then 
shifts to the defendant employer to come forward with legitimate non-
discriminatory reasons for its actions that negate the inference of 
retaliation. See Goldsmith, 996 F.2d 1155, at 1163. The plaintiff must 
then demonstrate that it will be able to establish at trial that the

employer's proffered non-discriminatory reasons are a pretextual ruse 
designed to mask retaliation.  Isenbergh v. Knight-Ridder Newspaper

           LEXIS 18981 (11th Cir. 1997).  
 
In this case, Plaintiff was not separated from his employment in retaliation for 
making a request for an accommodation. Plaintiff was separated from his 
employment after he exhausted all forms of leave available and did not return to 
work at Food City. Plaintiff would not consider alternatives to bringing his service 
dog to work and even after being provided a leave of absence, he did not indicate 
any inclination in returning to work unless it was with his service dog. There is no 
evidence that Food City retaliated against Plaintiff in violation of the ADA. Rather, 
the undisputed material facts simply show an employee who refused to return to 
work at Food City unless he could bring a service dog to work with him.  
Plaintiff was a satisfactory employee and Food City had no intent or desire to 
separate Plaintiff from employment, as evidenced by Food City’s efforts to engage 
Plaintiff in three (3) separate meetings concerning the issues related to bringing his 
dog to work. Food City was certainly not unwilling to explore reasonable 
accommodations with Plaintiff, but Plaintiff was only willing to return to work if 
Food City agreed to the accommodation he proposed. When Plaintiff failed to return 
to work at Food City after exhausting his leaves of absence, Food City removed him 
from employment.

IV. CONCLUSION  
Plaintiff never requested that he be provided a reasonable accommodation 
from Food City in order to address a disability; instead, after satisfactorily 
performing his job for two (2) years with no issues, Plaintiff arrived at work one day 
along with his service dog. Plaintiff stated the service dog was needed for stability 
and he essentially refused to work at any position unless he could do so with his dog.  
Food City conducted three (3) separate managerial meetings with Plaintiff in 
an attempt to address alternative accommodations with Plaintiff but he has 
repeatedly testified that he made it clear to Food City that he would not accept any 
position with Food City that did not include bringing his service dog to work. As 
such, this was not a request for a reasonable accommodation; rather, the presence of 
Plaintiff’s service dog was an unwavering condition precedent to continuing his 
employment with Food City, regardless of this job.   
Food City complied with all the requirements under the ADA by initiating and 
engaging in the interactive with the Plaintiff and by attempting to identify and offer 
alternative accommodations to Plaintiff.   Based on the undisputed material facts as 
set forth in Defendant’s accompanying Statement of Material Facts which is 
incorporated herewith, and the applicable law, Food City is entitled to summary 
judgment as a matter of law as to all of Plaintiff’s claims.
