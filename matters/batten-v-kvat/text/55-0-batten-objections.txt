I. INTRODUCTION 
This action arises under the Americans with Disabilities Act (ECF No. 1 at p. 
1). At specific issue in this case is whether the Plaintiff, Matthew Batten, was entitled 
to an accommodation of his disability, but at larger issue is whether this Court will 
propagate an interpretation of precedent that contradicts both the remedial purpose 
and the plain text of the ADA while punishing disabled persons specifically for their 
efforts to perform their jobs despite their physical limitations.  
The Report at issue here makes several critical errors. First, it ignores on-point 
persuasive precedent while simultaneously interpreting distinguishable precedent in 
such a way that it contradicts the clear statutory language and remedial purpose of 
the ADA. Second, it improperly ignores credible evidence from which a jury could 
rule in favor of the Plaintiff. In so doing, it violates the clear mandate that the 
evidence in the record be viewed in “the light most favorable to the non-moving 
party,” Schoenfeld v. Babbitt, 168 F.3d 1257, 1264 (11th Cir. 1999), and that all

Neurology, P.C., No. 1:19-cv00885-SCJ at *6 (N.D. Ga. 2021).

III. STANDARD OF REVIEW 
A district court reviews de novo all portions of a magistrate judge’s 
recommendation to which a party has properly objected. See Fed. R. Civ. P. 72. The 
district court “may accept, reject, or modify, in whole or in part, the findings or 
recommendations made by the magistrate judge,” or may “receive further evidence 
or recommit the matter to the magistrate judge with instructions.” 28 U.S.C. § 
636(b)(1). “De novo review of those portions of the magistrate’s report and findings 
to which a party timely objects is mandated by statute . . . and [is] crucial to the 
constitutionality of the Federal Magistrate Act, as amended.” Taylor v. Farrier, 910 
F.2d 518, 520 (8th Cir. 1990); see also, 12 Charles A. Wright & Arthur R. Miller, 
Federal Practice and Procedure § 3070.2 (2d ed. 2017) (a district judge “must not 
. . . rubber stamp” a magistrate’s facts and legal conclusions on de novo review). 
A court may grant summary judgment only if the moving party “shows that 
there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(a). “[S]ummary 
judgment will not lie if . . . a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the nonmoving 
party.” Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986). The moving party 
bears the initial burden of showing there is no genuine issue of material fact. Celotex 
Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 323 (1986). Inferences from the facts are viewed in

the light most favorable to the nonmoving party. United States v. Diebold, Inc., 369 
U.S. 654, 655 (1962).  
IV. ARGUMENT 
A. Summary Judgement in Favor of Defendant on Plaintiff’s Claim of ADA 
Failure to Accommodate is Unwarranted. 
1.

simultaneously interpreting distinguishable precedent in such a way that it 
contradicts the clear statutory language and remedial purpose of the ADA. 
 
Under the ADA, a “qualified individual” is defined as “an individual who, 
with or without reasonable accommodation, can perform the essential functions of 
the employment position that such individual holds or desires.” 42 USCS § 12111(8) 
(emphasis added). The black letter law unambiguously contemplates a “qualified 
individual” who is able to perform the essential functions of his job even without 
accommodation. Defendant’s argument to this Court, and the interpretation adopted 
by the Report, would effectively render that language meaningless by requiring this 
Court to find that a disabled person who can perform his job without accommodation 
is not a qualified individual under the ADA. In other words, the application proposed 
by the Report would effectively amend the ADA to read that a “qualified individual” 
is only one who “with reasonable accommodation, can perform the essential 
functions of the employment position.” This interpretation is inconsistent with the 
remedial nature of the ADA, which is designed to ensure that “those with disabilities 
Case 4:22-cv-00179-WMR     Document 55     Filed 09/08/23     Page 7 of 25
8 
 
can fully participate in all aspects of society, including the workplace.” Willis v. 
Conopco, Inc., 108 F.3d 282, 285 (11th Cir. 1997) (per curiam). 
The Report relies primarily on four cases in reaching its conclusion: 
D’Onofrio v. Costco Wholesale Corp., 964 F.3d 1014 (11th Cir. 2020), cert. denied, 
141 S. Ct. 1435 (2021); Lucas v. W.W. Grainger, Inc., 257 F.3d 1249 (11th Cir. 2001); 
LaChance v. Duffy’s Draft House, Inc., 146 F.3d 832 (11th Cir. 1998); and Willis v. 
Conopco, Inc., 108 F.3d 282 (11th Cir. 1997) (per curiam).  
The Eleventh Circuit Court of Appeals recently evaluated all four of these 
decisions in the case of Beasley v. O’Reilly Auto Parts, 69 F.4th 744, 758-60, n.10 
(11th Cir. 2023).3  As the Report acknowledges, the Beasley decision “criticized 
D’Onofrio and its line of cases . . . .” (ECF No. 53 at p. 22). Specifically, The Beasley 
decision described the rule which arose from the D’Nofrio line of cases as 
“expansive” and “broad statements” that are “necessarily tethered to the facts of 
those cases.” Id. at 757. The Beasley decision further described the rule from those 
cases as “dicta” saying that “judicial decisions cannot make law beyond the facts of 
the cases in which those decisions are announced.” Finally, the Beasley Court noted 
that those “broad statements” from the D’Nofrio line of cases “are in tension with 
the text of the statute and the EEOC regulations that implement it.” Id.

D’Onofrio v. Costco Wholesale Corp 
The primary support for both the Defendant’s position and the Report’s 
conclusion is the decision from the Eleventh Circuit Court of Appeals in D’Onofrio 
v. Costco Wholesale Corp., 964 F.3d 1014 (11th Cir., 2020), cert. denied, 2021 U.S. 
LEXIS 826 (U.S. Feb. 22, 2021). In that case, the Court addressed the plaintiff, 
D’Onofrio’s, argument that she had been denied reasonable accommodations related 
to her disability as a deaf person. D’Onofrio, 964 F.3d at 1021. D’Onofrio claimed 
that although she had had no issues communicating with her managers for “many 
years,” she began to have communication issues with a new general manager in 
2012. Id. at 1016. The Court interpreted this to mean that the communication issues 
were “new” and related solely to the new manager. Id. at 1017.  
D’Onofrio made two requests for accommodations related to the 
communication issues with the new manager: (1) that the new manager be 
transferred, and (2) that all the other managers be trained on “deaf culture.”  Id. The

Court noted that “D’Onofrio did not ask . . . for any other specific accommodation 
that might help her improve her communications with [the new manager].” Id. The 
employer “responded appropriately to D’Onofrio’s two requests.” Id. at 1024.  The 
employer denied the request to transfer the new manager, which the Court declared 
a “legally permissible response” given that both the EEOC and 11th Circuit caselaw 
hold that such an action is not a reasonable accommodation. Id. Nevertheless, the 
employer promised D’Onofrio that it would work to improve communication with 
the new manager and followed that up with the installation of specialized equipment 
in the workplace to aid in her communication with all managers. Id. In response to 
the second request, the employer agreed to organize deaf-culture training. Id.   
D’Onofrio argued that these actions by her employer were not reasonable 
accommodations because she had requested “alternative accommodations that were 
not granted.” Id. at 1028. The Court, however, noted that “’D’Onofrio cites no 
evidence to show that she requested alternative accommodations that went 
unfulfilled.” Id. In fact, the Court ultimately affirmed the holding of the district court 
“because D’Onofrio cannot point to a ‘specific instance in which she needed an 
accommodation and was denied one.” Id. at 1032 (quoting Batson v. Salvation Army, 
897 F.3d 1320, 1326 (11th Cir. 2018)).

LaChance v. Duffy’s Draft House, Inc. 
 
In LaChance, the Eleventh Circuit reviewed a district court’s grant of 
summary judgment in favor of the defendant holding that the plaintiff was not a 
“qualified individual” under the ADA. LaChance v. Duffy’s Draft House, Inc., 146 
F.3d 832, 833 (11th Cir. 1998). The plaintiff suffered from a years-long history of 
regular seizures. Id. These seizures would cause the plaintiff to become dazed and 
disoriented such that at times he would simply “wander off.” Id. at 834. The plaintiff 
worked as a “line cook” which required that he “cook on a gas flat top grill, use a 
fryolator filled with hot grease, and use slicing machines.” Id. The plaintiff’s doctor 
testified that Mr. LaChance should be restricted from using such equipment. Id. The 
district court concluded that “LaChance was not a qualified individual because he 
could not perform the essential functions of the job without threat of harm to himself 
or others.” Id. The Court of Appeals affirmed the district court’s ruling, finding that 
Mr. LaChance had failed to produce evidence that “reasonable accommodations 
were available.” Id. at 836. 
Lucas v. W.W. Grainger, Inc. 
 
In Lucas, the Eleventh Circuit Court of Appeals reviewed a decision of this 
Court granting summary judgment to the defendant in an ADA discrimination case. 
Lucas v. W.W. Grainger, Inc., 257 F.3d 1249, 1252 (11th Cir. 2001). This Court had 
determined that the Plaintiff had “not established a triable issue as to whether he was 
Case 4:22-cv-00179-WMR     Document 55     Filed 09/08/23     Page 11 of 25
12 
 
disabled . . . ,” and therefore granted summary judgment in favor of the defendant. 
Id. at 1256. The Court of Appeals chose to assume without deciding that the plaintiff 
was disabled and then affirmed on separate grounds. Id.  The plaintiff there argued 
that there were five reasonable accommodations, job reassignments, that the 
defendant could have provided but failed to. Id. The Court of Appeals rejected all of 
them.  
Reassignment to some positions was not proper because either there were no 
vacancies or the reassignments would have been a promotion, which is not mandated 
by the ADA. Id. at 1257.  Reassignment to other positions was not proper because 
the plaintiff’s disability prevented him from performing physical labor, Id. at 1259, 
or his doctor’s restrictions would have required eliminating essential functions of the 
position. Id. at 1260. All of this meant that the plaintiff could not be considered 
“otherwise qualified,” because even with the requested accommodations, he could 
not perform the essential functions of the job.   
Willis v. Conopco, Inc. 
The Willis Court’s decision hinged on the Plaintiff's burden to “identify an 
accommodation that would allow her to perform her job duties.” Willis v. Conopco, 
Inc., 108 F.3d 282, 283 (11th Cir. 1997) (per curiam). There, the Court of Appeals 
ruled against the Plaintiff by finding that the Plaintiff had failed “to produce evidence 
. . . of the existence of any ‘accommodation’ at all . . . .” Id. at 287. As such, the

Court of Appeals affirmed the District Court’s finding that “no triable issue of 
material fact existed on whether Defendant could have made reasonable 
accommodations for Plaintiff’s disability.” Id. at 284.  
The plaintiff failed in her burden because she had identified only two possible 
accommodations, neither of which would have addressed her disability. The first 
accommodation Ms. Willis requested was re-assignment to a new position. Id. at 
283. The second accommodation requested by Ms. Willis was a modification to her 
workspace. Id. The Court found that Ms. Willis failed in her burden on the first 
because she presented “no competent evidence that any alternative position existed,” 
Id. at 286, and failed in her burden on the second because she testified in her 
deposition that even if the work-space accommodation had been provided, she would 
still have been unable to perform her job in that environment. Id. at 287.  
None of the above cases directly involve a situation where the plaintiff could 
perform the job, with great difficulty, without the accommodation, and where the 
accommodation would relieve the difficulty. Instead, the respective holdings of 
D’Onofrio, LaChance, Lucas, and Willis can be summed up as follows: A disabled 
employee is not entitled to accommodation of his disability where, even with the 
identified accommodation, he could not perform the essential functions of his job. 
See D’Onofrio v. Costco Wholesale Corp., 964 F.3d 1014, 1017 (11th Cir. 2020), 
cert. denied, 141 S. Ct. 1435 (2021) (finding that the plaintiff had not identified any

accommodations “that might help her” other than the ones the defendant had already 
granted); LaChance v. Duffy’s Draft House, Inc., 146 F.3d 832, 834 (11th Cir. 1998) 
(“LaChance had not requested any accommodation from [Defendant] . . . such that 
he could have still performed the essential functions of a line cook.”); Willis v. 
Conopco, Inc., 108 F.3d 282, 287 (11th Cir. 1997) (per curiam) (“The ADA, as far

Unlike in D’Onofrio, Mr. Batten did not suddenly develop brand new

accommodation existed that would “allow him to perform his job duties.” 
Specifically, Mr. Batten identified an accommodation for his disability that would 
have “enable[d] [him] to perform the essential functions of the job.” Id. at 835.

The Report would have this Court adopt a reading of Eleventh Circuit caselaw 
that will result in cases where disabled plaintiffs are able to establish every element 
of a prima facie case and yet be denied their day in court. (See infra pp. 19-21). There 
is, however, an interpretation of the D’Nofrio line of cases that avoids this 
inconsistent and unjust result. The law in the Eleventh Circuit recognizes that an 
accommodation “can qualify as ‘reasonable,’ and thus be required by the ADA, only 
if it enables the employee to perform the essential functions of the job.” When this 
is strictly applied to Mr. Batten’s case, the answer is clear—allowing him to work 
with his service dog would undoubtedly have enabled him to perform the essential 
functions of his job. The Report goes too far by impliedly adding that not only must 
the accommodation enable the employee to do his job, but he must be totally unable 
to perform the essential functions of his job without it.4 That critical addition has no 
support in the plain text of the ADA or the governing regulations.

2. While improperly applying Eleventh Circuit caselaw that is clearly 
distinguishable the Report ignored persuasive authority that is directly 
on-point.  
 
 In response to the Defendant’s Motion for Summary Judgment, Plaintiff cited 
to the decision of the Northern District of Alabama in Hollingsworth v. O'Reilly Auto. 
Stores, Inc., No. 4:13-CV-01623-KOB, 2015 U.S. Dist. LEXIS 10956 (N.D. Al. 
2015). That decision clearly stands for the proposition that an employer may be 
obligated to provide reasonable accommodation to an employee even if the 
employee has been able to perform the essential functions of his job without 
accommodation. Id. at *27. Nevertheless, the Report makes no mention of the 
Hollingsworth decision, much less offers any explanation of how the Report’s 
interpretation of the D’Onofrio line of cases is consistent with Hollingsworth. 
Unlike the D’Onofrio line of cases, Hollingsworth is directly on point. In the 
Hollingsworth case, the plaintiff brought a claim for failure to accommodate under 
the ADA. Hollingsworth, No. 4:13-CV-01623-KOB, 2015 U.S. Dist. LEXIS 10956, 
at *1. Mr. Hollingsworth had suffered significant injuries in a prior motorcycle 
accident that among other things affected his ability to ambulate. Id. at *3. The 
defendant sought summary judgment arguing that the plaintiff “did not need an 
accommodation because he was able to perform the essential functions of his job 
without an accommodation.” Id. at *26. The Court noted that although the plaintiff 
was able to perform the essential functions of his job, “doing so caused him 
Case 4:22-cv-00179-WMR     Document 55     Filed 09/08/23     Page 17 of 25
18 
 
significant pain . . . .” Id. at *27. The Court stressed that an employer “cannot avoid 
its obligation to provide a reasonable accommodation to [an employee] simply 
because he was able to endure the pain of performing his job.” Id. The Court found 
that this created a genuine issue of material fact for trial. Id. at 28.    
In the present case, Mr. Batten likewise suffered significant injuries in a 
motorcycle crash that, among other things, affected his ability to ambulate. (ECF 
No. 44 at ¶¶ 1-7; Batten Affidavit at ¶¶ 2, 17; Batten Dep. 21:5-11). Mr. Batten 
likewise suffered significant pain in his attempts to perform the essential functions 
of his job. (ECF No. 44 at ¶ 8; Batten Dep. 29:6-19). Mr. Batten pushed himself to 
fulfill his job requirements with Defendant despite his disabilities causing him 
constant pain and causing him to fall on numerous occasions throughout his 
employment. (ECF No. 44 at ¶ 9; Batten Dep. 31:20-32:13, 84:6-20). Mr. Batten’s 
physical struggle to perform his job remained consistent throughout his employment 
with Defendant. (Batten Dep. 34:20-25). 
The Hollingsworth decision is consistent with the approach taken by several 
other jurisdictions. See, e.g., Rauen v. United States Tobacco Mfg., 319 F.3d 891, 
896-97 (7th Cir. 2003) (holding that while an employee’s ability to perform all

Claim in Unwarranted.  
1. The Report fails to consider material evidence in the record on the issue 
of whether Mr. Batten can present a prima facie case of disability 
discrimination. 
 
To establish a prima facie case of disability discrimination under the ADA, a 
plaintiff need only show that: (1) he is disabled; (2) he was a “qualified individual” 
at the relevant time; and (3) he was discriminated against because of his disability, 
which includes “‘not making reasonable accommodations to the known physical or 
mental limitations of an otherwise qualified individual with a disability’ unless doing

The Report notes here that only the third element of the prima facie case is in

3. The Report Improperly Weighs the Evidence on the Issue of Whether 
Defendant Failed to Engage in the Interactive Process 
 
 
The Report concludes that “the undisputed material facts show that plaintiff 
caused a breakdown in the interactive process.” (ECF No. 53 at p. 30). In reaching 
this conclusion, the Report accepts as true the Defendant’s allegation that Mr. Batten 
caused a breakdown in the interactive process by imposing an “ultimatum.” (ECF 
No. 53 at pp. 29-31). In concluding that Mr. Batten “would accept no 
accommodation that did not include the presence of his service animal,” the Report 
ignores facts from which a reasonable jury could conclude that it was the Defendant 
who caused the breakdown in the interactive process, not Mr. Batten. 
The Report relies explicitly on the case of Stewart v. Happy Herman’s 
Cheshire Bridge, Inc., 117 F.3d 1278, 1286 (11th Cir. 1997). In the Stewart case, the 
plaintiff there caused a breakdown in the interactive process by refusing all five of 
the accommodations offered by her employer without providing “any substantive 
reasons as to why [the accommodations] were unreasonable given her medical 
needs.” Stewart, 117 F.3d at 1286-87.

In this case, it is undisputed that the Defendant offered Mr. Batten only one

only accommodation offered by the Defendant—he could not physically do it 
because of his disability. Specifically, he told Defendant that he could not perform

that Mr. Batten ended his discussion with Defendant by indicating he would wait to

offered any. A reasonable jury could find that Mr. Batten did not refuse to consider 
other options, because no other options were ever presented to him aside from the 
one that he was physically incapable of accepting.
