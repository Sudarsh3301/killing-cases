In the Report, the Magistrate Judge correctly considered and

A. The Magistrate Judge did not “dismiss” the Beasley decision.  
 
In Plaintiff’s Objections, he alleges the Magistrate Judge “dismissed” the 
Beasley decision. (ECF Doc. 53, p. 9.) This is not accurate. Rather, the Magistrate 
Judge specifically noted that the Beasley Court recognized that “judicial decisions 
cannot make law beyond the facts the cases in which those decisions are announced.” 
Report, Doc. 53, p. 23, quoting Beasley, 69 F. 4th at 765-57. The Magistrate further 
noted the Beasley panel held that, “[t]o the extent that those three opinions’ broad 
statements about essential job functions go beyond the facts of those cases, we are 
not bound by those statements.”  Report, Doc. 53, p. 23, quoting Beasley, 69 F. 4th 
at 760.

This treatment from the Magistrate Judge is important because it reflects the 
Magistrate Judge’s reasoned analysis and consideration that the underlying facts in 
Beasley are dissimilar to the case sub judice.   
In Beasley, the plaintiff was deaf and the Court of Appeals held there were 
genuine issues of material facts concerning whether the plaintiff's inability to 
understand or participate in work-related meetings due to his deafness adversely 
impacted his employment. There was no dispute in Beasley that the plaintiff was 
deaf and that he had had an interpreter present for his first in-person interview with 
O'Reilly. In fact, at the time he was hired, the plaintiff had signed a form with 
O’Reilly which stated that he needed an accommodation to perform the essential 
functions of that job. Beasley v. O’Reilly Auto Parts, 69 F.4th 744, 747 (11th Cir. 
2023). Later in his employment, the plaintiff in the Beasley case requested 
accommodations specifically 
related to his hearing deficit but those 
accommodations were either never provided or were denied. Beasley v. O’Reilly 
Auto Parts, 69 F.4th 744, 746-747 (11th Cir. 2023). As the Magistrate Judge has 
observed in the Report, in Beasley, supra, the employer argued that the 
accommodations were not necessary for the plaintiff to perform the essential 
functions of his job and denied the request for accommodations. (Report, p. 22).

B. The Hollingsworth decision is neither factually relevant nor binding.   
 
 
Plaintiff further claims the Magistrate Judge failed to consider the case of  
Hollingsworth v. O’Reilly Auto Stores, Inc. No. 4:13-cv-01623-KOB, 2015 U.S. 
LEXIS 10956, 2015 WL 412894 (N.D. Ala.2015), but Hollingsworth is not binding 
precedent on this case and is factually distinguishable.  
 
In Hollingsworth, supra, the plaintiff had complained to his employer that he 
was having difficulty lifting items over 60 pounds because the other employees on 
his team were not assisting him, which was a condition stated in his job description. 
The plaintiff testified he had complained to his employer about this issue. 
Hollingsworth, 2015 U.S. LEXIS 10956, *4-6. After leaving two messages with his 
employer regarding the need for an accommodation, Hollingsworth never received 
a return call from anyone in the O’Reilly HR department. Hollingsworth, 2015 U.S.

Plaintiff herein never reported any falls or performance issues to Food City 
during the two years he performed his job without incident. It was only when he 
obtained his service dog that he claimed to have difficulties performing his job. 
Based on relevant precedent in this jurisdiction, Food City is entitled to summary 
judgment on this issue. Edwards v. WellStar Med. Grp., LLC, 2022 U.S. App. 
LEXIS 21036, 2022 WL 3012297 (11th Cir. 2022); D’Onofrio v. Costco Wholesale, 
Corp. 964 F.3d 1014, 1022 (11th Cir. 2020). 
 
Moreover, Food City did not refuse to consider an accommodation to the 
Plaintiff. The ADA does not impose liability on an employer for failure to provide 
the accommodation particularly desired by an employee. Stewart v. Happy 
Herman’s Cheshire Bridge, 117 F.3d 1278, 1286 (11th Cir. 1997). Rather, the ADA 
only requires an employer, such as Food City, to engage in the interactive process 
and provide a reasonable accommodation. As the Court of Appeals noted in Stewart, 
supra, “Stated plainly, under the ADA a qualified individual with a disability is ‘not 
entitled to the accommodation of her choice, but only to a reasonable 
accommodation’." Stewart, supra, at 1286, quoting Lewis v. Zilog., 908 F. Supp. 
931, 948 (N.D. Ga. 1995). 
 
The Magistrate’s Report recommends Summary Judgment in favor of Food 
City based on the following: (1) the plaintiff did not require an accommodation to 
perform the essential functions of his job and therefore, Food City was not required

By his own admissions above, Plaintiff was not willing to consider any other

II. 
In the Report, the Magistrate Judge correctly considered and 
applied applicable precedent and Summary Judgment in favor of Food City on 
Plaintiff’s claim of retaliation under the ADA is appropriate.   
     
 
The undisputed material facts show that Food City did not terminate Plaintiff 
in retaliation for engaging in a protected activity under the ADA. Plaintiff argues 
that “a reasonable jury could quite reasonably conclude that, had Mr. Batten not 
requested the use of a service dog, the Defendant would never have placed him on 
Case 4:22-cv-00179-WMR     Document 56     Filed 09/22/23     Page 10 of 14
11 
 
leave or ultimately terminated his employment.” (Pl’s Objections, ECF Doc. 55, p. 
22). Contrary to Plaintiff’s assertion, this argument incorrectly states the standard to 
establish a claim of retaliation under the ADA, which the Magistrate Judge 
thoroughly addressed in the Report at page 32. (ECF 53, Report at page. 32).   
There is no evidence that Food City retaliated against the Plaintiff in violation 
of the ADA. Rather, the undisputed material facts simply show an employee (i.e. 
Plaintiff) who refused to return to work at Food City unless he could bring a service 
dog to work with him. Food City was certainly not unwilling to explore reasonable 
accommodations with Plaintiff, but Plaintiff was only willing to return to work if 
Food City agreed to the single accommodation he had proposed. When Plaintiff 
failed to return to work at Food City after exhausting his leave of absence, Food City 
removed him from employment. This does not constitute retaliation and the 
Magistrate Judge’s Report should be affirmed on this claim as well.

CONCLUSION 
 
Plaintiff fails to overcome the persuasive and binding authority of the  
Eleventh Circuit Court of Appeals in  D’Onofrio v. Costco Wholesale Corp, 964 
F.3d 1014 (11th Cir. 2020), cert. denied, 141 S. Ct. 1435 (2021); Lucas v. W. W. 
Grainger, Inc., 257 F.3d 1249 (11th Cir. 2001); LaChance v. Duffy’s Draft house, 
Inc. 146 F.3d 832 (11th Cir. 1998); and Willis v. Conopco, Inc., 108 F.3d 282 (11th 
Cir. 1997). Plaintiff fails to establish any evidence to support his allegations of 
discrimination and/or retaliation under the ADA.  
 
Based on the foregoing, Defendant requests that the District Court overrule 
Plaintiff’s Objections and (1) adopt the Report and Recommendation from the 
Magistrate Judge in all respects and (2) grant Defendant’s Motion for Summary 
Judgment as to all of Plaintiff’s claims.
