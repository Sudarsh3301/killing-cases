II. 
Legal Standard 
 
In reviewing an R&R, this Court makes a “de novo determination of those 
portions of the report or specified proposed findings or recommendations to which 
objection is made.” 28 U.S.C. § 636(b)(1). “Parties filing objections to a magistrate’s 
report and recommendation must specifically identify those findings objected to. 
Frivolous, conclusive, or general objections need not be considered by the district 
court.” United States v. Schultz, 565 F.3d 1353, 1361 (11th Cir. 2009) (citations 
omitted). If no specific objections are made or no objections are made at all, “the 
appropriate standard of review for the report and recommendation is clear error.” 
Lattimore v. Bank of Am., N.A., 2014 WL 11456272, at *1 (N.D. Ga. Feb. 10, 2014), 
aff'd sub nom. Lattimore v. Bank of Am. Home Loans, 591 F. App’x 693 (11th Cir. 
2015). As Defendant has filed timely objections to the R&R in this case, the Court 
reviews the challenged portions of the R&R de novo and the remainder of the R&R 
for clear error.

III. 
Discussion 
 
Pursuant to Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 56(a), summary judgment is 
proper when the record evidence shows that “there is no genuine issue as to any 
material fact and that the moving party is entitled to a judgment as a matter of law.” 
Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(a); Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986). A dispute 
is “genuine” if evidence suggests a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the non-
movant. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986). “An issue of 
fact is ‘material’ if, under the applicable substantive law, it might affect the outcome 
of the case.” Hickson Corp. v. N. Crossarm Co., Inc., 357 F.3d 1256, 1259 (11th Cir. 
2004).  
 
The moving party bears the burden of demonstrating that there is no genuine 
issue of material fact that should be decided at trial. Clark v. Coats & Clark, Inc., 
929 F.2d 604, 608 (11th Cir. 1991). If the moving party carries the initial burden, 
then the burden shifts to the non-moving party to demonstrate that there is an issue 
of material fact that precludes summary judgment. Id. Then, summary judgment is 
only appropriate if the non-moving party fails to meet its burden of presenting 
evidence sufficient to create a genuine issue of material fact as to an essential 
element of its claim. Celotex Corp., 477 U.S. at 323. On summary judgment, the 
Court views the evidence and any inferences that may be reasonably drawn in the

light most favorable to the non-movant. Adickes v. S.H. Kress & Co., 398 U.S. 144, 
158–159 (1970).  
A. Summary Judgment is Proper on Plaintiff’s Failure to Accommodate 
Claim. 
 
Plaintiff has filed several objections to the Magistrate Judge’s findings related 
to his failure to accommodate claim. Before turning to those objections, a discussion 
of the Eleventh Circuit standard governing failure to accommodate claims is 
necessary. 
 
“The ADA imposes upon employers the duty to provide reasonable 
accommodations for known disabilities unless doing so would result in undue 
hardship to the employer.” Holbrook v. City of Alpharetta, Ga., 112 F.3d 1522, 1526 
(11th Cir. 1997). To survive summary judgment, a plaintiff must produce evidence 
that “(1) she was a qualified individual with a disability; (2) she made a specific 
request for a reasonable accommodation; and (3) her employer . . . failed to provide 
a reasonable accommodation, or engage in the requisite interactive process in order 
to identify a reasonable accommodation.” D’Onofrio v. Costco Wholesale Corp., 
964 F.3d 1014, 1021 (11th Cir. 2020). In this case, there is no dispute that Plaintiff 
has satisfied the first two elements for purposes of summary judgment. [Doc. 31 at 
9; Doc. 50 at ¶ 60]. Thus, the only remaining issue is whether Defendant failed to 
Case 4:22-cv-00179-WMR     Document 57     Filed 12/08/23     Page 5 of 21
6 
 
provide Plaintiff a reasonable accommodation or failed to engage in an interactive 
process to identify a reasonable accommodation.  
 
42 U.S.C. § 12111(9)(B) provides that a “reasonable accommodation” may 
include:  
job restructuring, part-time or modified work schedules, reassignment 
to a vacant position, acquisition or modification of equipment or 
devices, appropriate adjustment or modifications of examinations, 
training materials or policies, the provision of qualified readers or 
interpreters, and other similar accommodations for individuals with 
disabilities. 
However, an employee is “not entitled to the accommodation of her choice, but only 
to a reasonable accommodation.” Stewart v. Happy Herman’s Cheshire Bridge, Inc., 
117 F.3d 1278, 1286 (11th Cir. 1997) (quoting Lewis v. Zilog, Inc., 908 F. Supp. 
931, 947 (N.D. Ga. 1995)). “The key is ‘reasonability,’ meaning an employer is not 
required to accommodate an employee in any manner that the employee desires—or 
even provide that employee’s preferred accommodation.” D’Onofrio v. Costco 
Wholesale Corp., 964 F.3d 1014, 1021 (11th Cir. 2020). 
 
To select an appropriate and reasonable accommodation, “it may be necessary 
for the [employer] to initiate an informal, interactive process with the individual . . . 
[to] identify the precise limitations resulting from the disability and potential 
reasonable accommodations that could overcome those limitations.” 29 C.F.R. § 
1630.2(o)(3). However, an employer is not liable for failure to accommodate when 
Case 4:22-cv-00179-WMR     Document 57     Filed 12/08/23     Page 6 of 21
7 
 
it “does not obstruct an informal interactive process[,] makes reasonable efforts to 
communicate with the employee and provide accommodations based on the 
information it possesses[,] and the employee’s actions cause a breakdown in the 
interactive process.” Stewart v. Happy Herman’s Cheshire Bridge, Inc., 117 F.3d 
1278, 1287 (11th Cir. 1997). Thus, “[u]nder the ADA, an employer will not be liable 
for failure to accommodate if the employee is responsible for the breakdown of the 
interactive process.” D’Onofrio v. Costco Wholesale Corp., 964 F.3d 1014, 1022 
(11th Cir. 2020). 
 
Moreover, the Eleventh Circuit has historically held that “if an employee does 
not require an accommodation to perform her essential job functions, then the 
employer is under no obligation to make an accommodation, even if the employee 
requests an accommodation that is reasonable and could be easily provided.” 
D’Onofrio, 964 F.3d at 1022; see also Lucas v. W.W. Grainger, Inc., 257 F.3d 1249, 
1255 (11th Cir. 2001) (“An accommodation can qualify as ‘reasonable,’ and thus be 
required by the ADA, only if it enables the employee to perform the essential 
functions of the job.”). However, as the Magistrate Judge’s R&R highlights, the 
Eleventh Circuit’s recent panel opinion in Beasley v. O’Reilly Auto Parts calls this 
principle into question. 69 F.4th 744 (11th Cir. 2023). Specifically, the Beasley 
majority wrote: 
When faced with the question of whether an ADA plaintiff was a 
“qualified individual” under 42 U.S.C. § 12112(a), we have sometimes

8 
 
made broad statements that “an accommodation is ‘reasonable’ and 
necessary under the ADA only if it enables the employee to perform 
the essential functions of the job.” Lucas, 257 F.3d at 1259–60 
(emphasis added); see also LaChance v. Duffy’s Draft House, Inc., 146 
F.3d 832, 835 (11th Cir. 1998) (“An accommodation is reasonable — 
and, therefore, required under the ADA — only if it enables the 
employee to perform the essential functions of the job.”) (quotation 
marks omitted). And in a case where the employer provided all the 
accommodations that were required under the ADA, we said that “if an 
employee does not require an accommodation to perform her essential 
job functions, then the employer is under no obligation to make an 
accommodation.” D’Onofrio, 964 F.3d at 1022. 
 
Those statements about essential job functions in those three decisions 
are expansive, but they are necessarily tethered to the facts of those 
cases. That is important because “whatever their opinions say, judicial 
decisions cannot make law beyond the facts of the cases in which those 
decisions are announced.” Pretka v. Kolter City Plaza II, Inc., 608 F.3d 
744, 762 (11th Cir. 2010) (quotation marks omitted); see also id. 
(“Statements in an opinion that are not fitted to the facts, or that extend 
further than the facts of that case, or that are not necessary to the 
decision of an appeal given the facts and circumstances of the case, are 
dicta. We are not required to follow dicta in our own prior decisions.”). 
Beasley, 69 F.4th at 756–57. Nevertheless, the Beasley majority did little to settle 
the debate, summing up its opinion by saying: “So what do we hold about whether 
the ADA’s requirement that an employer provide an employee with reasonable 
accommodations extends beyond those that will enable him to perform the essential 
functions of his job? Nothing, really. There is enough dicta on that subject already.” 
Id. at 760.

B. Summary Judgment is Proper on Plaintiff’s ADA Retaliation Claim. 
 
The Magistrate Judge recommended that Plaintiff’s retaliation claim be 
dismissed on two independent grounds. See [Doc. 53 at 32–34].  First, the Magistrate 
Judge found that Plaintiff failed to present evidence showing that Defendant 
terminated his employment because he sought accommodation. [Id. at 33]. The 
Magistrate Judge instead found that the undisputed facts show that Plaintiff was 
placed on leave after he informed Defendant that he would only accept an 
accommodation allowing him to use a service dog at work, and Defendant 
terminated Plaintiff’s employment when he exhausted his leave and had not returned

17 
 
to work. [Id.]. Second, the Magistrate Judge recommended that Plaintiff’s retaliation 
claim be dismissed because the facts supporting it are the same as those supporting 
his failure to accommodate claim. [Id. at 33–34].  
 
Plaintiff has not objected to the Magistrate Judge’s recommendation that his 
retaliation claim be dismissed because it is based on the same facts as his failure to 
accommodate claim. [Id.]. After reviewing the Magistrate’s finding on this point for 
clear error, the Court finds none. Accordingly, the Court adopts the Magistrate 
Judge’s finding as the opinion of this Court. Although this is a sufficient and 
independent ground for dismissing Plaintiff’s retaliation claim, the Court will also 
consider Plaintiff’s objections to the remainder of the Magistrate Judge’s findings as 
to his retaliation claim. Before considering those objections, a discussion of the 
standard governing retaliation claims is necessary. 
“To establish a prima facie case of retaliation, a plaintiff must show: (1) 
statutorily protected expression; (2) adverse employment action; and (3) a causal 
link between the protected expression and the adverse action.” Stewart v. Happy 
Herman’s Cheshire Bridge, Inc., 117 F.3d 1278, 1287 (11th Cir. 1997). In the 
context of requesting disability accommodations, a plaintiff engages in statutorily 
protected expression if he shows “that he had a good faith, objectively reasonable 
belief that he was entitled to [his requested] accommodations under the ADA.” 
Standard v. A.B.E.L. Servs., Inc., 161 F.3d 1318, 1328 (11th Cir. 1998). If the 
Case 4:22-cv-00179-WMR     Document 57     Filed 12/08/23     Page 17 of 21
18 
 
plaintiff establishes a prima facie case, “the burden then shifts to the defendant 
employer to come forward with legitimate non-discriminatory reasons for its actions 
that negate the inference of retaliation.” Stewart, 117 F.3d at 1287. If the employer 
produces such evidence, “[t]he plaintiff must then demonstrate that it will be able to 
establish at trial that the employer’s proffered non-discriminatory reasons are a 
pretextual ruse designed to mask retaliation.” Id.  
 
There is no dispute here that Plaintiff has satisfied the first two elements of a 
prima facie retaliation claim by engaging in statutorily protected expression 
(requesting an accommodation) and suffering an adverse employment action (having 
his employment terminated). Thus, the remaining issues are causation and the 
burden-shifting analysis. See Stewart, 117 F.3d at 1287. As it relates to causation, 
the Magistrate Judge found, and Plaintiff contests, that “there is no probative 
evidence that plaintiff was separated from employment because he sought 
accommodation.” [Doc. 53 at 33]. Plaintiff objects that the Magistrate Judge 
“ignore[d] a clear chain of admissions” from which a reasonable jury could conclude 
that, “had [Plaintiff] not requested the use of a service dog, Defendant would never 
have placed him on leave or ultimately terminated his employment.” [Doc. 55 at 21–
22]. 
 
The Court agrees with Plaintiff that he has produced sufficient evidence to 
show a causal connection between his request for accommodation and his firing.

[Doc. 55 at 21–22]. “To establish a causal connection, a plaintiff must show that the 
relevant decisionmaker was ‘aware of the protected conduct, and that the protected 
activity and the adverse actions were not wholly unrelated.’” Kidd v. Mando Am.

make a showing of “but-for causation.” Frazier-White v. Gee, 818 F.3d 1249, 1258

so that he could “reconsider and engage in further discussion with Food City to 
identify other alternatives that would meet his needs besides the service dog, such 
as the USCAN position.” [Doc. 43 at ¶ 38]. The undisputed facts further show that 
Defendant terminated Plaintiff “for failing to return from leave.” [Doc. 50 at ¶ 75]. 
In other words, but for Plaintiff’s request for an accommodation, he would not have 
been placed on leave, and but for his failure to return from that leave, he would not 
have been fired. Thus, in a technical sense, the Court cannot say that Plaintiff’s 
request for accommodation and his firing were “wholly unrelated.” Kidd, 731 F.3d 
at 1211.

and Plaintiff has failed to “demonstrate that it will be able to establish at trial that 
the employer’s proffered non-discriminatory reasons are a pretextual ruse designed 
to mask retaliation.” Stewart, 117 F.3d at 1287. Plaintiff and Defendant agree that 
Plaintiff “was terminated by Defendant effective February 14, 2022, for failing to 
return from leave.” [Doc. 50 at ¶ 75].6 Plaintiff’s failure to return to work (i.e., his 
absenteeism) is a legitimate and non-discriminatory reason to terminate his 
employment. See Cunningham v. Nature’s Earth Pellets, L.L.C., 433 F. App’x 751, 
753 (11th Cir. 2011) (finding that the plaintiff’s “history of absenteeism” was a  
legitimate reason to terminate her employment); Wills v. Postmaster General, 300 
F. App’x 748, 752 (11th Cir. 2008) (“Here, the [employer] offered [the employee’s] 
excessive absences as a legitimate, nondiscriminatory explanation for its actions, 
and, therefore, [the employee] was required to demonstrate that that reason was 
pretextual.”). Plaintiff has failed to introduce any evidence that “meet[s] that reason
