II. 
SUMMARY JUDGMENT STANDARD 
 
 
The granting of a motion for summary judgment is “only appropriate when 
‘there is no genuine issue as to any material fact and … the moving party is entitled 
to a judgment as a matter of law.’” Schoenfeld v. Babbitt, 168 F.3d 1257, 1264 (11th 
Cir. 1999) (emphasis added) (quoting Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c); Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 
477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986)). “A genuine issue of material fact exists when ‘the 
evidence is such that a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the non[] moving 
party.’” Rogers v. Mentor Corp., 682 Fed. Appx. 701, 708 (11th Cir. 2017) (quoting 
Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986)). In examining the record 
on such a motion, the Court views the evidence in “the light most favorable to the 
non-moving party.” Schoenfeld, 168 F.3d at 1264, and resolves all reasonable doubts 
in favor of the non-moving party. Change v. Midtown Neurology, P.C., No. 1:19-cv-
00885-SCJ, 2021 U.S. Dist. LEXIS 115658, at *6 (N.D. Ga. 2021). Granting a 
motion for summary judgment is only appropriate “if a case is ‘so one-sided that one 
party must prevail as a matter of law.’” Rogers, 682 Fed. Appx. at 708 (quoting 
Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 251-52 (1986).

APPLICABLE LAW 
 
McDonnel Douglas  
 
 
In employment discrimination and retaliation cases, Plaintiffs may rely on 
direct or indirect evidence of discrimination to support their claims. de la Cruz v.

Children's Trust, 843 F. Supp. 2d 1273, 1281 (S.D. Fla. 2012). Where a plaintiff 
relies on indirect (circumstantial) evidence, the claim is generally subject to the 
burden-shifting analysis outlined by the United States Supreme Court in McDonnell 
Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792 (1973). Wilson v. B/E Aero., Inc., 376 F.3d 
1079, 1087 (11th Cir. 2004). Direct evidence is defined as “evidence which reflects 
‘a discriminatory or retaliatory attitude correlating to the discrimination or retaliation 
complained of by the employee.’” Id. at 1086 (quoting Damon v. Fleming 
Supermarkets of Fla., Inc., 196 F.3d 1354, 1358 (11th Cir. 1999)). It is evidence that 
“if believed, proves [the] existence of [a] fact without inference or presumption.” Id. 
(quoting Burrell v. Bd. of Trustees of Ga. Military Coll., 125 F.3d 1390, 1393 (11th 
Cir. 1997)).  
 
If a plaintiff presents sufficient evidence to establish a prima facie case, then 
the burden shifts to the Defendant to provide a “a legitimate, nondiscriminatory 
reason for its adverse actions. Davidson v. CHSPSC LLC, 861 Fed. Appx. 306, 311 
(11th Cir. 2021). The Plaintiff must then establish that the Defendant’s stated reason 
was false and that the real reason was discrimination. Id. This “pretext” can be shown 
by 
demonstrating 
“‘such 
weaknesses, 
implausibilities, 
inconsistencies, 
incoherencies, or contradictions in [the employer's] proffered legitimate reasons for 
its action that a reasonable factfinder could find them unworthy of credence.’” Id.

(quoting Springer v. Convergys Customer Mgmt. Grp. Inc., 509 F.3d 1344, 1348 
(11th Cir. 2007)).  
 
Significantly, Courts in the 11th Circuit hold that the McDonnell Douglas 
framework “is not, and was never intended to be the sine qua non for a plaintiff to 
survive summary judgment in an employment discrimination case.” Id. (quoting 
Smith v. Lockheed-Martin Corp., 644 F.3d 1321, 1328 (11th Cir. 2011)). Such a 
plaintiff may survive summary judgment if he presents “a convincing mosaic of 
circumstantial evidence that would allow a jury to infer intentional discrimination 
by the decisionmaker.” Id. (quoting Smith v. Lockheed-Martin Corp., 644 F.3d 1321, 
1328 (11th Cir. 2011)). Among “other things,” this “mosaic” may consist of evidence 
showing “(1) suspicious timing, ambiguous statements, and other bits and pieces 
from which an inference of discriminatory intent might be drawn, (2) systemically 
better treatment of similarly situated employees, and (3) that the employer’s 
justification is pretextual.” Id. (quoting Lewis v. City of Union City, Ga., 934 F.3d 
1169, 1185 (11th Cir. 2019)).  
 
The 11th Circuit Court of Appeals has held that the McDonnell Douglas 
framework does not govern claims of disability discrimination under the ADA, 
including failure to accommodate.  Nadler v. Harvey, No. 06-12692, 2007 U.S. App. 
LEXIS 20272, at *28 (11th Cir. 2007). Therefore, on such claims, once the Plaintiff 
has proffered sufficient evidence to support a prima facie case, “summary judgment

ADA Discrimination 
 
In the 11th Circuit, to establish a prima facie case of ADA discrimination, a 
plaintiff must show that he “(1) was disabled; (2) was a qualified individual; and (3) 
was discriminated against because of [his] disability.” Davidson, 861 Fed. Appx. at 
310. A “qualified individual” is defined as “an individual who, with or without 
reasonable accommodation, can perform the essential functions of the employment 
position that such individual holds or desires.” 42 USCS § 12111(8). This requires a 
plaintiff to establish “either that he can perform the essential functions of his job 
without accommodation, or . . . that he can perform the essential functions of his job 
with a reasonable accommodation.” McKane v. UBS Fin. Servs., Inc., 363 Fed. Appx. 
679, 681 (11th Cir. 2010) (emphasis added) (quoting Davis v. Fla. Power & Light 
Co., 205 F.3d 1301, 1305 (11th Cir. 2000)).  
Failure to Accommodate  
 
 
In the 11th Circuit, a failure by an employer to provide reasonable 
accommodation to a disabled person is itself a form of discrimination under the ADA 
“so long as that individual is ‘otherwise qualified,’ and unless the employer can show 
undue hardship.” Holly v. Clairson Indus., L.L.C., 492 F.3d 1247, 1262 (11th Cir. 
2007). To establish a prima facie case of ADA discrimination by failure to

accommodate, a plaintiff must show that “(1) he is disabled; (2) he is a qualified 
individual; and (3) he was discriminated against by way of the defendant’s failure to 
provide a reasonable accommodation.” McKane v. UBS Fin. Servs., Inc., 363 Fed. 
Appx. 679, 681 (11th Cir. 2010).  
 
A “reasonable accommodation” is defined to include: 
(A) making existing facilities used by employees readily accessible to 
and usable by individuals with disabilities; and 
(B) job restructuring, part-time or modified work schedules, 
reassignment to a vacant position, acquisition or modification of 
equipment or devices, appropriate adjustment or modifications of 
examinations, training materials or policies, the provision of qualified 
readers or interpreters, and other similar accommodations for 
individuals with disabilities.

A requested accommodation is “reasonable and required only if it enables the 
employee to perform an essential function of the job.” McKane, Inc., 363 Fed. Appx. 
at 681 (quoting Lucas v. W.W. Grainger, Inc., 257 F.3d 1249, 1255 (11th Cir. 2001)). 
This is true, even if the employee has been able in the past to perform the essential 
functions of his job without accommodation. (See infra pp. 18-19).

employment, a plaintiff must show that he (1) engaged in statutorily protected 
conduct, (2) suffered an adverse employment action, and (3) that there is a causal 
connection between the two. Todd v. Fayette Cty. Sch. Dist., 998 F.3d 1203, 1219

LEGAL ARGUMENT 
 
A. Plaintiff is a “Qualified Individual” Under the Law and Entitled to 
Reasonable Accommodation even Though he had Performed his job 
Without it. 
 
Defendant argues to this Court that Plaintiff’s request for the use of his service 
animal was “not a reasonable and required accommodation under the ADA because 
it was not necessary in order for Plaintiff to perform the essential functions of the 
fuel clerk position.” (Defendant’s Brief in Support of Defendant K-VA-T’s Motion 
for Summary Judgment, p. 12). Defendant’s argument on this point is without merit 
because the governing statutes and caselaw clearly establish that a Plaintiff is not 
ineligible for reasonable accommodation under the ADA simply because he has been 
able to perform the essential functions of his job without it. 
Under the ADA, a “qualified individual” is defined as “an individual who, 
with or without reasonable accommodation, can perform the essential functions of 
the employment position that such individual holds or desires.” 42 USCS § 12111(8) 
(emphasis added). The black letter law unambiguously contemplates a “qualified 
individual” who is able to perform the essential functions of his job even without an

accommodation. Defendant’s argument to this Court would effectively render that 
language meaningless by requiring this Court to find that a disabled person who can 
perform his job without accommodation is not a qualified individual under the ADA.  
Defendant further distorts the caselaw that they cite for this argument. 
Defendant recites the basic premise that “An accommodation is reasonable and 
necessary under the ADA only when it enables the employee to perform the essential 
functions of the position.” (Defendant’s Brief in Support of Defendant K-VA-T’s 
Motion for Summary Judgment, p. 12). For this premise, Defendant cites to three 
cases: McKane v. UBS Fin. Servs., Inc., 363 Fed. Appx. 679, 681 (11th Cir. 2010); 
Holly v. Clairson Industries, LLC, 492 F.3d 1247, 1255 (11th Cir. 2007); and Lucas 
v. W.W. Grainger, Inc., 257 F.3d 1249 (11th Cir. 2001). Defendant, however, omits a 
crucial phrase that explains the above premise:  
“If the individual is unable to perform an essential function of his job, 
even with an accommodation, he is, by definition, not a ‘qualified 
individual’ and, therefore, not covered under the ADA. In other words, 
the ADA does not require the employer to eliminate an essential 
function of the plaintiff's job.”  
 
Holly v. Clairson Industries, LLC, 492 F.3d 1247, 1255 (11th Cir. 2007). See also, 
McKane v. UBS Fin. Servs., Inc., 363 Fed. Appx. 679, 681 (11th Cir. 2010) (“Thus, 
if [the plaintiff] is unable to perform an essential function of his . . . job, even with 
an accommodation, he is, by definition, not a qualified individual and, therefore, not 
covered under the ADA.”).

Taken together, the statutory authority and the above-cited caselaw establish 
that a plaintiff must show that he is able to perform the essential functions of his job 
because the law does not require the employer to provide an accommodation that, 
even if granted, would not enable the employee to perform his job.  
Defendant’s main support for their argument is the decision of the 11th Circuit 
Court of Appeals in D’Onofrio v. Costco Wholesale Corp., 964 F.3d 1014, 1022 
(11th Cir., 2020), cert. denied, 2021 U.S. LEXIS 826 (U.S. Feb. 22, 2021). In that 
case, the Court addressed the plaintiff, D’Onofrio’s, argument that she had been 
denied reasonable accommodations related to her disability as a deaf person. 
D’Onofrio, 964 F.3d at 1021. D’Onofrio claimed that although she had had no issues 
communicating with her managers for “many years,” she began to have 
communication issues with a new general manager in 2012. Id. at 1016. The Court 
interpreted this to mean that the communication issues were “new” and related solely 
to the new manager. Id. at 1017.  
D’Onofrio made two requests for accommodations related to the 
communication issues with the new manager: (1) that the new manager be 
transferred, and (2) that all the other managers be trained on “deaf culture.”  Id. The 
Court noted that “D'Onofrio did not ask . . . for any other specific accommodation 
that might help her improve her communications with [the new manager].” Id. The 
employer “responded appropriately to D’Onofrio’s two requests.” Id. at 1024.

C. Plaintiff’s Requested Accommodation was Reasonable and Posed no

Defendant argues that Mr. Batten was not entitled to the accommodation he 
“particularly desired”—only a reasonable accommodation. (Defendant’s Brief in 
Support of Defendant K-VA-T’s Motion for Summary Judgment, p. 14). There is 
ample evidence in the record from which a reasonable jury could conclude that Mr. 
Batten’s requested accommodation was not only a reasonable accommodation, but 
perhaps the only reasonable accommodation.  
Under the applicable regulations pertaining to the ADA, a “service animal” is 
defined as “any dog that is individually trained to do work or perform tasks for the

D. A Reasonable Jury Could Conclude that Defendant Retaliated Against 
Plaintiff for Requesting an Accommodation of his Disability. 
 
There is sufficient evidence in the record to establish a prima facie case of 
ADA retaliation. The first element requires proof of statutorily protected conduct. 
Mr. Batten requested a disability accommodation, which is statutorily protected 
conduct. See Jeter v. Dixie, No. 1:13cv197-MEF, 2013 U.S. Dist. LEXIS 128185, at

*10 (M.D. Al. 2013). The Second element requires proof of an adverse employment 
action. Mr. Batten was placed on a leave of absence and then terminated from his 
job. (Plaintiff’s Statement of Additional Material Facts, ¶ 75). The third and final 
element requires proof of a causal link between the first two elements. Mr. Batten’s 
being placed on leave was in connection to his request for the use of his service dog 
and specifically the Defendant’s denial of that request. (Defendant’s Statement of 
Material Facts, ¶ 38). And Mr. Batten’s termination was for his failure to return from 
that leave. (Plaintiff’s Statement of Additional Material Facts, ¶ 75).  
A reasonable jury could conclude that there was a causal connection between 
Mr. Batten’s firing and his initial request for accommodation. As such, this creates a 
genuine issue for trial and Defendant’s Motion for Summary Judgment on this issue 
must be denied.

genuine disputes as to material facts. As such, Plaintiff respectfully asserts that 
Defendant’s Motion for Summary Judgment must be denied.
